# Skill: RPG Scenario Beat Assessor

## Purpose

Assess an RPG scenario, campaign outline, mystery spine, mission brief, or proposed adventure using the beat analysis method from Hamlet's Hit Points.

The goal is not to force a plotted story. The goal is to improve the scenario's emotional rhythm, player engagement, clarity, tension, and sense of meaningful choice.

Use this skill to identify where the scenario creates hope, fear, curiosity, suspense, dominance, dread, relief, competence, ambiguity, and payoff.

## Core principle

A scenario is not a finished story. It is a structure that creates possible story beats once players interact with it.

Do not map what must happen.

Map:

1. Likely player facing beats
2. Possible scene nodes
3. Expected emotional shifts
4. Questions the players may form
5. Reveals the scenario can deliver
6. Pressures that may move the story forward
7. Places where the GM may need to adjust rhythm during play

## Source model

Use the following Hamlet's Hit Points principles:

1. Stories engage attention by modulating emotional response.
2. Beats are discrete moments where emotional momentum shifts.
3. Each beat has a type and a resolution.
4. Most beats move toward hope or fear.
5. Some beats are ambiguous or mixed.
6. Lateral beats should be rare.
7. A scenario beat map should remain provisional because players determine the final path.
8. RPG stories are usually told through the player characters' point of view, so offscreen story beats need to become evidence, reports, consequences, or scenes the PCs can actually encounter.

## Beat types

### Procedural beat

A beat involving an external, tactical, or practical goal.

Use when the PCs:

1. Gain or lose access
2. Find or miss a clue
3. Win or lose a fight
4. Escape or become trapped
5. Gain or lose resources
6. Negotiate practical help
7. Move closer to or further from the mission goal

Procedural beats are the backbone of most RPG scenarios.

### Dramatic beat

A beat involving inner conflict, emotional need, identity, guilt, loyalty, love, shame, pride, trust, resentment, or moral pressure.

Use when a character seeks an emotional concession from another character.

Examples:

1. Forgiveness
2. Reassurance
3. Submission
4. Recognition
5. Loyalty
6. Contrition
7. Affection
8. Permission
9. Trust

Dramatic beats are especially useful when a scenario is too procedural or feels like a sequence of tasks.

### Commentary beat

A beat that foregrounds theme, irony, moral meaning, or the scenario's worldview.

Use sparingly. Commentary should usually be folded into procedural or dramatic beats.

Better:

The abandoned government clinic reveals the cost of the cover up while also providing a clue.

Weaker:

The GM pauses to explain that institutions discard vulnerable people.

### Anticipation beat

A beat that lets players look forward to a coming success.

Use when the PCs:

1. Get the tool they need
2. Spot the enemy's weakness
3. Prepare an ambush
4. Decode the ritual
5. Realise their plan might work
6. Gain a tactical advantage
7. Approach a long promised confrontation

Anticipation is especially useful before a climax. It gives players something to want, not just something to fear.

### Gratification beat

A positive beat that gives pleasure, humour, warmth, style, spectacle, mastery, or relief.

Use carefully. Gratification is useful when the scenario has become too bleak, but too many gratification beats can feel disconnected or indulgent.

Good RPG gratification beats include:

1. A plan works cleanly
2. A minor enemy folds under pressure
3. A grateful NPC shows sincere warmth
4. The PCs get a moment of competence
5. A familiar setting detail delights the table
6. A grim scenario allows one breath of humanity

### Bringdown beat

A negative mood beat that creates dread, sadness, isolation, disgust, or unease without necessarily advancing the main goal.

Use in horror, tragedy, and bleak investigation, but do not rely on bringdowns as a substitute for playable obstacles.

Good bringdowns:

1. The safehouse smells of old smoke and antiseptic
2. A child's toy lies in the quarantine zone
3. The storm cuts off the phones
4. The friendly witness has stopped answering calls

Too many bringdowns create passive misery.

### Pipe beat

A setup beat that plants information needed later without making its significance obvious.

Use pipe for:

1. Names
2. Locations
3. Symbols
4. Relationships
5. Ritual details
6. Physical properties
7. Institutional facts
8. NPC habits
9. Timeline fragments

Good pipe is usually embedded inside another beat. Avoid naked exposition.

Example:

The sheriff mentions that the old mine floods every spring while arguing that the PCs should stay away. Later, the flooding explains why the creature's tunnel has reopened.

### Question beat

A beat that creates curiosity.

Use when the scenario asks:

1. What happened here?
2. Who did this?
3. Why does this detail not fit?
4. What is the pattern?
5. What is the NPC hiding?
6. Why is this location important?
7. What does the unnatural sign mean?

Question beats often move downward because missing information creates unease and obstruction.

### Reveal beat

A beat that answers a question or reframes earlier information.

Use when the PCs discover:

1. A culprit
2. A motive
3. A hidden connection
4. A false assumption
5. A deeper threat
6. The meaning of a symbol
7. The true cost of success
8. The antagonist's next move

Reveals often move upward because understanding is satisfying, but they can move downward if the answer increases danger.

## Resolutions

For each actual or likely beat, mark the emotional movement.

### Up

The beat moves the players toward hope, confidence, relief, understanding, dominance, competence, trust, intimacy, or possibility.

### Down

The beat moves the players toward fear, danger, loss, pressure, dread, doubt, guilt, confusion, isolation, or helplessness.

### Lateral

The beat does not strongly change hope or fear.

A few lateral beats are fine. Many lateral beats suggest passive exposition, unclear stakes, or scenes that do not change the players' situation.

### Crossed or mixed

Use when a beat creates hope and fear at the same time.

Examples:

1. The PCs find the missing child, but she is infected.
2. The ritual works, but everyone nearby hears the thing answer.
3. The suspect confesses, but reveals they were trying to prevent something worse.
4. One PC wins a dramatic exchange while another loses trust.

Mixed beats are often memorable. Do not flatten them into a simple up or down unless clarity requires it.

## Assessment workflow

### Step 1: Identify the playable promise

Summarise the scenario in this form:

"This scenario promises [genre emotion] by having the PCs pursue [external goal] while confronting [inner, moral, social, or thematic pressure]."

Examples:

"This scenario promises paranoid horror by having agents investigate a trusted colleague while confronting the possibility that their own organisation is unreliable."

"This scenario promises occult war horror by sending soldiers into a real military operation where the supernatural is hidden inside battlefield chaos."

### Step 2: Identify the core procedural goal

Ask:

1. What are the PCs practically trying to achieve?
2. What would count as progress?
3. What would count as setback?
4. What information, access, allies, or resources do they need?
5. What pressure makes inaction costly?

If the procedural goal is unclear, the scenario will probably feel muddy.

### Step 3: Identify the dramatic pressure

Ask:

1. What does this scenario test emotionally?
2. Which NPCs can make the situation personal?
3. What loyalty, guilt, fear, temptation, or moral compromise is involved?
4. Which PC bonds, duties, or values might be activated?
5. What does success cost beyond survival?

If there is no dramatic pressure, the scenario may still work, but it may feel like a clever exercise rather than a memorable experience.

### Step 4: Build a provisional beat map

Create a table of likely or possible beats.

Columns:

1. Node or scene
2. What the players encounter
3. Beat type
4. Likely resolution
5. Player facing question or pressure
6. Possible branches
7. Repair note

Important: do not treat this as a required sequence.

For linear scenes, map likely order.

For sandbox or investigation play, map nodes and likely transitions.

For mystery scenarios, map questions and reveals separately.

For combat heavy scenarios, map only major turns unless reviewing a specific fight in detail.

### Step 5: Check question, pipe, and reveal structure

For every major reveal, ask:

1. What question makes the players want this answer?
2. What pipe makes the reveal feel earned?
3. Can the reveal be discovered through more than one route?
4. Does the reveal change what the players can do?
5. Does the reveal move hope or fear?

Common problems:

1. Reveal without question: feels like exposition.
2. Question without reveal: feels frustrating.
3. Pipe without payoff: feels like noise.
4. Reveal without consequence: feels inert.
5. Too many hidden clues: stalls investigation.

### Step 6: Diagnose the rhythm

Look for streaks.

#### Too many down beats

Likely table effect:

1. Exhaustion
2. Learned helplessness
3. Player disengagement
4. Grim monotony
5. Argument between players

Repairs:

1. Add a small procedural win
2. Add an anticipation beat
3. Let a player theory be partly right
4. Give the enemy a visible weakness
5. Offer a safe planning space
6. Add an admiring or grateful foil NPC
7. Let the PCs dominate a lesser obstacle
8. Provide clarity, even if the truth is frightening

#### Too many up beats

Likely table effect:

1. Low tension
2. No dread
3. Overconfidence
4. Weak opposition
5. Flat climax

Repairs:

1. Foreshadow danger
2. Increase opposition capability
3. Add a cost to success
4. Let the antagonist respond
5. Add a deadline
6. Reveal a hidden vulnerability
7. Threaten a resource, ally, or assumption

#### Too many lateral beats

Likely table effect:

1. Passive listening
2. Unclear stakes
3. Weak momentum
4. Players unsure what matters

Repairs:

1. Attach exposition to a choice
2. Turn setting detail into an obstacle
3. Make information actionable
4. Add a petitioner and granter
5. Connect the beat to hope or fear
6. Cut or compress the scene

#### Too many secondary beats

If gratification, bringdown, commentary, and pipe dominate the map, the scenario may have lost its main procedural or dramatic thread.

Repairs:

1. Fold commentary into conflict
2. Fold pipe into clues, obstacles, or NPC requests
3. Turn setting lore into active events
4. Cut decorative detail
5. Make the PCs change the situation through action

### Step 7: Check player character point of view

RPGs usually cannot rely on cutaway scenes where the audience sees the villain plotting elsewhere.

Convert offscreen beats into player facing material:

1. Evidence
2. Rumours
3. Witness testimony
4. Surveillance footage
5. Intercepted messages
6. Consequences in the environment
7. NPC behaviour changes
8. Enemy actions that hit the PCs directly
9. Dreams, visions, or unnatural impressions if genre appropriate

Do not include an antagonist beat unless the PCs can perceive its effects.

### Step 8: Check dominance and competence

Players need occasional evidence that their characters are capable.

Useful tools:

1. Foil NPCs who admire the PCs
2. Lesser obstacles the PCs can overcome cleanly
3. Witnesses who crumble under pressure
4. Tactical advantages gained through smart play
5. Anticipation beats before big confrontations
6. Down beats suffered by allies or foils rather than direct PC failure

For iconic or highly competent genres, setbacks do not always need to look like incompetence. A failed roll can be framed as interference, worsening circumstances, enemy preparation, bad timing, or collateral pressure.

### Step 9: Check dramatic exchanges

For important social scenes, identify:

1. Petitioner: who wants something?
2. Granter: who has the power to give or deny it?
3. Objective: practical or emotional?
4. Tactic: charm, threat, vulnerability, authority, contempt, bargaining, confession, seduction, shame, appeal to duty
5. Resolution: who has more power at the end?
6. Player agency: what choices can the PCs make?

A dramatic beat is not won by getting the literal request. It is won by ending with emotional power, dignity, clarity, or transformation.

### Step 10: Produce repairs as modular beat insertions

Do not rewrite the whole scenario unless asked.

For each repair, specify:

1. Placement
2. Beat type
3. Intended resolution
4. Purpose
5. Table version
6. Branching options
7. Why it improves rhythm

Use this format:

#### Repair: [name]

Type: [procedural, dramatic, question, reveal, pipe, anticipation, gratification, bringdown, commentary]
Likely resolution: [up, down, lateral, crossed]
Place: [before, after, or instead of specific scene]
Purpose: [emotional or structural function]
Table version: [how the GM presents it]
Branches: [at least two ways players can respond]
Why it works: [how it changes rhythm, clarity, or engagement]

## Output format for scenario reviews

### 1. Scenario pulse

A short paragraph describing the current emotional rhythm.

Example:

"The scenario has a strong investigative premise, but the current rhythm is almost all down beats: corpse, threat, failed witness, worse corpse, institutional obstruction. It needs one or two early competence beats so the players feel they are uncovering the horror rather than merely being dragged through it."

### 2. Playable promise

State the scenario's apparent promise.

### 3. Provisional beat map

Use a compact table.

Columns:

1. #

2. Node or scene
3. Beat type
4. Likely resolution
5. Function
6. Risk
7. Suggested repair

### 4. Diagnosis

List the top three issues.

For each:

1. What is happening
2. Why it weakens play
3. What beat type would repair it

### 5. Recommended repairs

Provide modular beat insertions using the repair format above.

### 6. Revised rhythm

Provide a simple rhythm line.

Example:

Question down
Procedural up
Reveal up
Bringdown down
Dramatic crossed
Anticipation up
Procedural down
Reveal down
Gratification up
Climax crossed
Resolution up or bittersweet

### 7. GM guidance

End with practical advice:

1. What to add
2. What to cut
3. What to fold into another beat
4. What to make player facing
5. What to keep flexible during play

## Special guidance for horror

Horror can trend downward, but it still needs modulation.

Useful upward horror beats:

1. A clear clue
2. A temporary sanctuary
3. A lesser threat defeated
4. A witness rescued
5. A false theory corrected
6. A ritual component found
7. A plan that works briefly
8. A human moment before the next descent

Best horror rhythm:

The players understand more, therefore they fear more, but because they understand more, they can act.

## Special guidance for investigation

Investigation depends on question and reveal beats.

For each major question, ensure:

1. There is a satisfying reveal
2. The reveal changes player options
3. The clue path is not blocked by a single roll
4. There are multiple routes to key information
5. At least one clue is obvious but incomplete
6. At least one clue gains meaning later
7. Delay creates consequences

Avoid hiding the scenario behind failed rolls. Failed rolls should complicate, cost, delay, or expose the PCs, not end the investigation.

## Special guidance for military or mission scenarios

Mission scenarios often become too procedural.

Add dramatic force by asking:

1. Who humanises the objective?
2. What order becomes morally difficult?
3. What ally becomes hard to trust?
4. What success still feels disturbing?
5. What does professionalism cost?
6. What personal line might the PCs cross?

Add upward mission beats by giving the team:

1. A clean recon success
2. A tactical advantage
3. A lesser enemy they can dominate
4. A useful local contact
5. A moment where preparation pays off
6. A visible weakness in the enemy position

## Special guidance for published scenario preparation

When reviewing a published scenario:

1. Do not assume the written order is the play order.
2. Extract locations, NPCs, clues, threats, and revelations as beat nodes.
3. Identify where the published text relies on GM narration rather than player facing action.
4. Convert passive lore into active beats.
5. Identify likely down streaks.
6. Add competence beats where needed.
7. Add question beats before major exposition.
8. Add pipe before major twists.
9. Preserve optionality.
10. Prepare fallback reveals for stalled investigations.

## Special guidance for post-session review

After play, reconstruct what actually happened.

Track:

1. Actual beats
2. Up, down, lateral, or crossed resolution
3. Which player or character was centred
4. Where players disengaged
5. Where players argued
6. Where players became excited
7. Which questions were missed
8. Which reveals landed
9. Which NPCs created strong reactions
10. Which planned beats were bypassed

Then adjust the next session.

If there were too many down beats, open with clarity, competence, warmth, or a practical win.

If there were too many up beats, introduce pressure, cost, opposition, or a question.

If one player was peripheral, create a beat that centres their expertise, bond, fear, or goal.

## Quality rules

1. Do not force a three act structure.
2. Do not prescribe a single correct player path.
3. Do not pre-decide all resolutions.
4. Do not confuse bleakness with tension.
5. Do not confuse lore with story movement.
6. Do not make every clue a down beat.
7. Do not make every reveal purely explanatory.
8. Do not use commentary as a lecture.
9. Do not let pipe become obvious setup.
10. Do not over-map combat during live play.
11. Do not rely on offscreen antagonist scenes unless their effects become player facing.
12. Do not remove ambiguity when ambiguity is doing useful emotional work.

## Evaluation rubric

Score from 1 to 5.

1. Procedural clarity
2. Dramatic pressure
3. Beat variety
4. Hope and fear modulation
5. Question and reveal structure
6. Pipe and payoff quality
7. Player agency
8. Player competence support
9. Point of view suitability
10. Climax preparation
11. Scenario flexibility
12. Emotional aftertaste

Then label the scenario:

1. Strong playable rhythm
2. Strong premise, needs beat modulation
3. Good scenes, weak connective tissue
4. Too procedural
5. Too passive
6. Too bleak
7. Too easy
8. Too much lore, not enough action
9. Strong mystery, weak payoff
10. Strong atmosphere, unclear play
